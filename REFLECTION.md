# Individual Technical Reflection

**Student:** Oluwaleimu Oluwasegun (301865517)
**Course:** MIT 8103 Advanced Database Systems
**Case study:** ShopNaija Online Marketplace

The design decision I’m most confident about is using JSONB to store product attributes instead of creating a separate column for every possible feature. Different products have different needs: a phone has storage and RAM, a dress has size and material, and rice has weight. Creating fixed columns for all these attributes would leave many fields empty. JSONB offered a more flexible solution while still allowing efficient searches through a GIN index. I applied similar thinking in Portfolio 4, where I recommended using different databases for different purposes. PostgreSQL handles payments and stock because these require strong constraints and reliable transactions across multiple rows. MongoDB, on the other hand, is better suited to the product catalogue and order tracking, where information is often accessed as complete documents.

Some of my biggest lessons came from the problems I encountered along the way. For example, my constraint-testing script reported an error even though the database had correctly prevented a delete operation. The issue was that PostgreSQL returned a different error name for the RESTRICT rule than my script expected. This taught me not to assume that every test failure means the system is broken. Sometimes, I need to examine the actual error message to understand what happened. In Portfolio 2, I also had to verify that my original execution plans had been preserved after creating indexes. Without a genuine baseline, comparing performance before and after optimisation would not have been reliable. I encountered another challenge in MongoDB, where an incorrectly written validation rule prevented the entire data-loading process because the rule was checked when the collection was created.

The distributed database tests helped me understand concepts that had previously seemed theoretical. Watching an order wait for 12.7 seconds while the replica set elected a new primary, without losing any orders, gave me a practical understanding of availability and retryable writes. I was also surprised to find that `w: "majority"` was slower than `w: 3`. Investigating this behaviour helped me understand how waiting for writes to be journaled to disk affects performance and durability, and why stronger guarantees can come at the cost of speed.

Looking back, I would approach a real-world project by agreeing on the data model and defining the evidence needed to validate the system before starting implementation. I would also commit smaller pieces of work more frequently throughout the project, rather than leaving too much until later. These experiences have taught me that good database design is not just about making things work; it is also about testing assumptions, understanding unexpected behaviour, and being able to demonstrate that the solution is reliable.
